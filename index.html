Mapping Malaysia’s Public Transport Demand and Access

Explore how population concentration becomes transport demand, how riders move through national and RapidKL networks, and where cost, transfers, and last-mile access shape everyday mobility.

Where People Gather

Public transport demand begins with the uneven geography of people. This section first identifies where Malaysia’s population is most concentrated, then narrows from the state level to individual districts to reveal which areas are most likely to generate sustained urban mobility pressure.

Population Concentration by State

This establishes the demand base for the dashboard. States with larger populations are more likely to require frequent, high-capacity public transport, especially where urban growth concentrates trips into a small number of dense corridors.

Synthesised Insight

Malaysia’s transport demand is shaped by concentrated population centres, not an even national spread. The state chart gives the broad demand picture, while the district ranking shows whether that demand is driven by many places or by a few dominant urban districts.

This matters because dense areas such as Selangor, Kuala Lumpur, and Putrajaya are more likely to generate repeated everyday trips for work, education, and services.

Largest Districts and the Rank-Size Drop-off

The ranking highlights how quickly population size falls after the largest districts. A steep drop-off suggests that transport planning cannot rely only on state totals, because a few districts may carry a disproportionate share of potential ridership demand.

From Population to Movement

Population concentration shows where demand could exist, but ridership reveals where people are actually travelling by public transport. This section shifts from potential demand to observed movement, comparing national transport modes and then examining how KTMB services vary in typical usage and seasonal patterns.

National Ridership Trends by Transport Mode

This chart shows how public transport usage changes over time across broad modes. It helps identify whether movement is recovering, growing, or fluctuating, and whether rail and bus services contribute differently to the national ridership pattern.

KTMB Ridership Distribution by Service

The distribution view compares not only which KTMB services carry more riders, but also how stable or inconsistent their daily demand is. Wider spreads indicate services where ridership is more variable, which may reflect weekday–weekend differences, seasonal travel, or uneven service reliance.

KTMB Monthly Ridership Pattern

Normalising each service against its yearly average makes the comparison fairer across services of different sizes. The focus is not just total volume, but whether each service experiences months of unusually high or low demand that may affect planning and resource allocation.

RapidKL Rail Movement Structure

The national view shows broad movement patterns, but RapidKL origin-destination data reveals the internal structure of rail travel within the urban network. This section examines where passengers start and end their journeys, separating dense station-pair patterns from the strongest corridor-level flows.

RapidKL Station-Pair Ridership Matrix

The matrix makes repeated station-pair demand visible by comparing origins and destinations in the same view. Darker cells point to stronger travel relationships, while the diagonal is treated as non-movement because a station-to-itself pair does not represent an actual passenger corridor.

Busiest RapidKL Passenger Corridors

The flow map turns the strongest station-pair relationships into a spatial story. Thick lines highlight the corridors carrying the most passengers, while colour helps distinguish whether demand stays within the same rail line or crosses between different parts of the RapidKL system.

The Cost of Movement

High ridership does not automatically mean a journey is easy. Accessibility is also shaped by the fare paid, the time spent travelling, and whether passengers must transfer between services. This section connects movement patterns to the practical burden experienced by riders.

Fare and Travel Time by RapidKL Journey

This comparison shows whether longer trips also tend to cost more, and whether transfer journeys occupy a different part of the fare–time space. It helps reveal journeys that may be less convenient even when they are technically connected by the network.

Transfer Penalty on Travel Time

Transfers can create a hidden burden beyond the fare itself. By comparing typical travel time and variability, this chart shows whether journeys requiring transfers are consistently longer or less predictable than direct journeys.

The Last-Mile Question

A transport network is only useful if people can reach it from where they live, study, or work. This final section moves from rail corridors to local access infrastructure, using Cyberjaya bus stops as a case study for how last-mile coverage supports or limits public transport connectivity.

Cyberjaya Bus Stop Locations and POI Access

The map shows whether bus stops are positioned near identifiable points of interest or spread across less clearly connected areas. This helps evaluate whether local stops are likely to support meaningful access to daily destinations, rather than simply existing as isolated points on the road network.
